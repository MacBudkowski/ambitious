# Range: borrow a concrete technique from another field

**Use when** the usual approach keeps failing the same way, or the task's surface discipline is too narrow for what it's really trying to do.

## How to apply it

1. **Replace the noun with a verb.** An onboarding guide isn't "a document". It's "getting a new user to their first success without help". Saving articles isn't "a bookmark list". It's "remembering an idea at the moment you start relevant work". The verb shows which fields have something to offer.
2. **Find where it breaks down.** "How have you approached this so far, and where does it fall short?" Reuse answers already given.
3. **Pick the field and the technique yourself.** The user shouldn't have to invent the cross-disciplinary link. Introduce one concrete technique in one or two sentences, then ask how it could fit.
4. **Borrow methods, not costumes.** Don't ask the model to be a short seller. Do what a short seller does to research a position. Role-play makes the transcript stranger without improving the result.

## Example borrowings

| Problem as a verb | Field | Technique |
|---|---|---|
| Getting users through a flow without confusion | Human–computer interaction | Cognitive walkthrough of each decision point |
| Remembering ideas when they matter | Learning design | Task-linked cues and retrieval questions at the start of work |
| Making busy people believe a cause-and-effect story | Journalism / editing | Inverted pyramid; lead with the change and its cause |
| Finding where a plan is wrong | Finance / forensics | Short-seller or forensic-accountant red-team analysis |
| Combining conflicting advice | Research synthesis | Group by method, then check whether disagreement reflects different contexts |

These are illustrations, not proof that a technique works for this task.

## Good suggestion shape

> "Before rewriting the guide again, we could run a cognitive walkthrough, a usability technique: step through each screen asking 'will the user know what to do here, and will they know it worked?'. That usually finds the exact decision points where people get lost. That's Range ambition: borrowing a method from another field, here interaction design. It finds likely problems; only real users confirm them."

## When combining several sources

Compare their underlying advice rather than listing titles. Group similar views, check whether apparent disagreement reflects different circumstances, and ask the question that decides which fits: "Some of these say prototype early, others say research first. What's less clear here: whether you can build it, or whether people want it?" Keep attribution, and don't treat repeated claims as independent confirmation.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Instead |
|---|---|
| "Imagine a panel of experts…" | Name one technique and what it does |
| Asking the user which field to borrow from | Propose one; ask how it fits |
| A lecture on the borrowed field | One or two sentences, then a question |
| Claiming a model simulation proves real user behavior | Say what it finds and what still needs real users |

Range
In short, being ambitious in range means that we consider our work from the perspective of different fields — more fields than would have been economical in the past. This isn’t a particularly unique insight, every single heist movie spends the first 30 minutes assembling a diverse cast of characters and talents. As AI products make it easier and easier to run multi-agent systems (with multiple threads, orchestrators, or simply subagents), I’ve seen really interesting results by imploring Claude to bring in additional fields.

Putting this into practice isn’t easy; it requires you to be creative. Until I saw my colleague Alex Albert use Blender to render interior designs, I never considered using Claude for 3D environments for my homepage — even though I’ve previously made video games! However, this is easily where I see the most magic happen.

So, how do we do this? I use the following techniques:

Try to replace the nouns with verbs: An old trick from The Art of Innovation by Tom Kelley and Jonathan Littman. A quarterly review isn’t “a deck”. It’s “getting busy people to believe a story about cause and effect, and to decide under uncertainty, in 30 minutes”. Having the verbs makes it easier for both me and Claude to understand which fields might bring in a unique and useful perspective.
Assemble the crew: The deck needs to be amazing, otherwise aliens will blow up Earth. What kind of team will humanity get together? For production quality, I’d bring in an analyst, a journalist, and an editor. If it has to be presented, probably a motion and audio designer, too. It might be useful to employ an adversarial “red team”, too, so let’s bring in a short seller and a forensic accountant.

It is easy to accidentally engage in role play, which will make your transcript weirder without improving the actual outcome. Use this method to find fields and techniques. Don’t ask the model to write a short seller, ask it to do what a short seller does to research their position.

All the ways in which I describe ambition require the model to have a computer with the ability to install software and access to all kinds of relevant data (either using MCP connectors or other means). Plenty of tools make this possible and I recommend that you use one.
